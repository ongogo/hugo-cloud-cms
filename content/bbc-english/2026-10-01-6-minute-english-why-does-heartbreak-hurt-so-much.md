## 本周问题

> **Which theory, developed by psychologists in the 1950s, describes a link between children's early bonds with their parents and their romantic relationships as adults?**
> 
> 1950s 心理学家提出的哪个理论描述了儿童早期与父母的纽带与成年后恋爱关系的联系？
> 
> a) contraction theory (收缩理论)
> 
> b) **attachment theory** (依恋理论) ✅
> 
> c) superstition theory (迷信理论)

**答案**：**b) attachment theory (依恋理论)**。Georgie 回答正确。

## 课文重点解析

### 1. 核心问题：Why does heartbreak hurt so much?

> **Neil**: *"The pain you feel is not just imaginary – it registers in the same part of the brain that processes physical pain."*
> 
> **Neil**: *"你感受到的痛苦不是想象的——它在处理躯体疼痛的同一脑区被记录下来。"*

---

### 2. 恋爱大脑：生存系统与「上瘾」

> **Dr Lucy Brown**: *"This is a survival system. Nature gave us these systems to be **addicted to each other** – for reproduction, for protection. You're **orientated completely toward the other person**. It's like hunger or thirst."*
> 
> **Dr Lucy Brown**: *"这是**生存系统**。大自然让我们**彼此上瘾**——为了繁衍、保护。你**完全朝向对方**。就像饥饿或口渴。"*

**关键发现**：
| 现象 | 大脑机制 | 进化功能 |
|------|----------|----------|
| **看着恋人照片** | 激活进食/饮水提醒区 | 生存驱动 |
| **恋爱「上瘾」** | 多巴胺奖赏系统 | 促进结对 |
| **相互 orientated** | 注意力偏向伴侣 | 保护投资 |

---

### 3. 恋爱改变自我认同

> **Neil**: *"A 2013 study showed... People were shown statements about themselves... the part of the brain we use to think about ourselves lit up. When showing the same statements about their partner, **the same part of the brain was active** — not the part we generally use to think about others."*
> 
> **Neil**: *2013 研究：看关于自己的陈述 → 自我脑区亮起。看关于伴侣的同样陈述 → **同一自我脑区**亮起，而非思考他人的脑区。*

**含义**：伴侣被纳入 **self-concept (自我概念)**，成为「延伸的自我」。

---

### 4. 社会纽带 = 长寿预测因子 #1

> **Prof. Zoe Donaldson**: *"Having really strong **social bonds** increases your likelihood that you'll survive from just about anything... overall is the **number one predictor of longevity**. So, if you are lonely... your mortality risk goes up in a way that's equivalent to being obese or smoking."*
> 
> **Prof. Zoe Donaldson**: *强**社会纽带**让你几乎从任何事中存活几率增加……是**长寿头号预测因子**。孤独无纽带，**死亡率上升相当于肥胖或吸烟**。*

| 因素 | 死亡率风险相当于 |
|------|------------------|
| **孤独/无社会纽带** | 肥胖、吸烟 |
| **强社会纽带** | 癌症、抑郁症存活率↑ |

---

### 5. 失恋 = 真实的脑部疼痛

> **Prof. Zoe Donaldson**: *"It's **literal pain in your brain**. If you look at the parts of the brain that light up, they **overlap with the same brain regions** that are activated when you feel **visceral pain** — when you feel actual... like you get a burn, or you break a bone."*
> 
> **Prof. Zoe Donaldson**: *这是大脑中的**字面疼痛**。亮起脑区与**躯体内脏疼痛**（烧伤、骨折）**重叠**。*

**神经科学证据**：
- fMRI 显示：*anterior cingulate cortex (前扣带回)*、*insula (岛叶)* 同时激活
- *social pain (社会性疼痛)* 共用 *physical pain (躯体疼痛)* 神经回路
- 解释为何失恋感到 *"chest pain" (胸痛)、"stomach pain" (胃痛)*

---

## 批判性思考题

1. **社会性疼痛的进化逻辑**：*social pain* 共用 *physical pain* 回路，体现 *"social baseline theory" (社会基线理论)*——人类大脑预设社会连接为默认基线。失恋 = *baseline deviation* (偏离基线)。如何用英语表达 *"social thermostat, allostatic load, predictive coding"*？

2. **依恋类型与失恋反应**：*attachment theory* 分 *secure, anxious, avoidant, disorganized* 四类。*anxious attachment* 失恋后 *"protest behavior" (抗议行为)*、*hyperactivation*；*avoidant* 则 *"deactivation"*。如何用英语描述 *"attachment injury, earned security"*？

3. **爱作为「成瘾」的神经伦理**：*romantic love = behavioral addiction* (行为成瘾)，共享 *mesolimbic dopamine pathway*。若开发 *"anti-love drug" (抗恋爱药物)* 治疗病态失恋，*informed consent, authenticity, identity* 伦理困境如何解决？*neuroethics*

4. **孤独流行病的公共卫生策略**：*loneliness mortality risk = smoking/obesity*，但公共卫生投入极不对等。*social prescribing (社会处方)*、*community connectors*、*digital companionship* 如何量化 *cost-effectiveness*？*QALY, DALY* 计算。

5. **Ex 的认知重构**：*ex-partner* 从 *self-inclusive* → *other-exclusive* 的神经重组需要时间。*memory reconsolidation (记忆再巩固)* 窗口期干预、*narrative identity reconstruction* 如何用英语表达？*closure, meaning-making, post-traumatic growth*。

**Georgie**: And do scientists know why being in love impacts our brain in this way?

**Neil**: So, it's all about survival. Our brains have evolved to be this way. Dr Lucy Brown explains more to the BBC World Service.

**Georgie**: Lucy says our brains make us **addicted to** our romantic partners. If you're **addicted to** something, you want more of it. You can literally be **addicted to** things like drugs or alcohol, but we also use it metaphorically to talk about things we love.

**Neil**: She says that romantic partners are **orientated to** each other, which in British English we say **orientated**, and it means they are pointed towards each other. They think of each other before other people.

**Georgie**: Neil, is there anything else scientists have discovered from scanning the brains of people in love?

**Neil**: Well, being in love doesn't just change the way we think. It changes our whole identity. A 2013 study showed us more about this. People were shown statements about themselves – for example, Neil is kind – and the part of the brain we use to think about ourselves lit up. When showing the same statements about their partner, the same part of the brain was active – not the part of the brain we generally use to think about others.

**Georgie**: So, Neil, are all of these brain changes that come with being in love actually good for us?

**Neil**: Well, studies suggest that good social connections help manage stress and support our immune system. Professor Zoe Donaldson explained more to the BBC World Service.

**Professor Zoe Donaldson**: Having really strong **social bonds** increases your likelihood that you'll survive from just about anything, whether that be cancer, depression, etc. And it overall is the **number one predictor of longevity**. So, if you are lonely and you don't have bonds, then your mortality risk goes up in a way that's equivalent to being obese or smoking.

**Georgie**: So, having strong **social bonds** or close relationships with other people makes you more likely to survive disease, according to Zoe.

**Neil**: Yes, it's an important factor in our **longevity**, which means how long we live. In fact, Zoe says, being lonely and not having good **social bonds** increases your mortality risk, your risk of dying, by as much as being obese or smoking.

**Georgie**: OK. So, what happens when these bonds are broken, when we experience **heartbreak**?

**Neil**: Well, scientists have been looking at this by asking people to look at photos of their **exes**, their former partners, and scanning their brains. They found that **heartbreak** activates parts of the brain we use when we're in danger or in pain. Professor Zoe Donaldson explains more.

**Professor Zoe Donaldson**: It's **literal pain in your brain**. So, if you look at the parts of the brain that light up, they **overlap with the same brain regions** that are activated when you feel **visceral pain** – when you feel actual, you know... like you get a burn, or you break a... you break a bone.

**Neil**: OK. So, now it's time for the answer to the quiz question I asked you. It was about a theory developed by psychologists in the 1950s, which says that children's early relationships with their parents impact their relationships as adults. What's the name of the theory?

**Georgie**: I was quite confident about this, wasn't I, Neil? I said **attachment theory**.

**Neil**: Well, you had a reason to be confident because you are correct.

**Georgie**: Yay! OK. Let's recap the vocabulary we learnt in this programme, starting with **heartbreak**, which is the feeling you experience after you lose someone you love.

**Neil**: If you're **addicted to** someone or something, you want more of it all the time.

**Georgie**: **Oriented** or **orientated towards** is used metaphorically to mean that someone or something is your main focus.

**Neil**: **Social bonds** are the close relationships we have with family, friends and romantic partners.

**Georgie**: **Longevity** means how long you'll live.

**Neil**: And an **ex** is a past romantic partner that you are no longer in a relationship with.
