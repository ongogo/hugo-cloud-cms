## 课文重点解析

### 1. 核心话题：人车沟通难题

> **Stephen Brewster**: *"If you're outside a vehicle right now, you can see the driver inside the vehicle, you can wave to them, they can see you, you can make eye contact. But when you come to an autonomous vehicle, there's no person in the driving seat, so how do you negotiate whether they're going to go, whether they're going to let you go?"*
> 
> **斯蒂芬·布鲁斯特教授**：*"现在你在车外，能看到车里司机，能冲他们挥手、进行眼神交流。但面对自动驾驶车辆，驾驶座没人，你怎么'协商'它是走还是让你先走？"*

**关键挑战**：
- ✅ 传统沟通：**eye contact (眼神接触)** + **wave (挥手示意)**
- ✅ 无人车缺失人类驾驶员 → 需要**替代沟通界面**
- ✅ **negotiate (协商通行权)** 成为关键动词

---

### 2. 技术方案：多模态警示系统

| 用户群体 | 适合方案 | 原因 |
|----------|----------|------|
| **行人** | **Animations (动画)** | 有时间观看动态显示（如绿色行人走动） |
| **跑步者/骑行者** | **Coloured light rings (彩色灯光环)** | 速度快，仅能 **glance (匆匆一瞥)**，类交通灯最有效 |

> **Chris Vallance**: *"What kind of signals worked in terms of alerting pedestrians and runners and cyclists to the intentions of the vehicle?"*
> 
> **克里斯·瓦伦斯**：*"什么信号能有效**警示**行人、跑步者、骑行者车辆意图？"*

> **Stephen Brewster**: *"The animations – they work well for pedestrians, but when it came to runners and cyclists, they have much less time to make their decisions, so if you do a quick glance at a vehicle, you don't really see the animation."*
> 
> **布鲁斯特教授**：*"动画对行人有效，但跑步者/骑行者决策时间极短，**匆匆一瞥**根本看不清动画。"*

---

### 3. 文化因素：Jaywalking 与自动驾驶适配性

> **Ammar Al-Taie**: *"In South Korea, for example, jaywalking is illegal, whereas in the UK, you know, we tend to jaywalk a lot."*
> 
> **阿马尔·阿尔-泰伊**：*"韩国**禁止闯红灯**，而英国人经常乱穿马路。"*

**洞察**：
- ✅ **Jaywalking** = 美式英语，指"非法/不安全横穿马路"
- ✅ 法规严格、行人守规矩的文化（如韩国） → 自动驾驶更安全、更易部署
- ✅ 英国拟晚些时候引入无人车 → 真实考验即将到来

## 批判性思考题

1. **人机交互设计**：无人车缺失"眼神接触+挥手"这一人类进化出的协商协议。设计替代方案时，如何平衡**普适性**（跨文化理解）、**即时性**（毫秒级反应）、**冗余度**（多通道备份）？

2. **多模态警示的认知负荷**：行人看动画、骑行者看灯环——这种**用户分层界面**是否会增加认知负荷？如何通过用户测试验证？

3. **文化-技术协同演化**：韩国"禁闯红灯"文化使自动驾驶更易落地。技术部署是否会倒逼交通文化变迁（如更严格执法、道路设计标准化）？

4. **责任归属与伦理**："Negotiate whether they're going to let you go" 暗示车辆拥有"决定权"。当沟通失败导致事故，责任在车企、算法、行人、还是监管者？

5. **词汇扩展 - 交通法规术语**：*jaywalking, right of way, crosswalk, pedestrian crossing, traffic calming, shared space, vulnerable road users*。梳理"共享道路"相关法律词汇体系。

### Transcript

**Phil**: Hello, this is 6 Minute English from BBC Learning English. I'm Phil.

**Pippa**: And I'm Pippa.

**Phil**: Here at 6 Minute English, we love discussing new tech. One of the most controversial technologies of recent years is driverless cars, also known as self-driving cars, autonomous cars or robotaxis.

**Pippa**: Many people say they wouldn't feel safe in a car without a human driver, but there are concerns from other road users too – pedestrians, runners and cyclists. How will driverless cars interact with them? It's a problem Professor Stephen Brewster, from Glasgow University, has been working on. He explained more to BBC World Service programme Tech Life.

**Stephen Brewster**: If you're outside a vehicle right now, you can see the driver inside the vehicle, you can wave to them, they can see you, you can make eye contact. But when you come to an autonomous vehicle, there's no person in the driving seat, so how do you negotiate whether they're going to go, whether they're going to let you go?

**Phil**: On my walk to work, I need to cross the road. Usually, a driver makes **eye contact** with me. They look directly into my eyes and then **wave** me across. To **wave** means to raise your hand and move it from side to side. It's a gesture for saying hello, or a way of telling a person to do something.

**Pippa**: Luckily, Phil, you usually arrive safely at work and as yet there are no driverless cars in London. But, Phil, how would you feel about crossing that road in front of a robotaxi?

**Phil**: Well, maybe a little bit nervous. I might wait for it to go and go behind it or something.

**Pippa**: Mmm... yeah, I get that. I think maybe as we get more used to these technologies, we'll get more used to how to deal with them and when to cross. But in this episode, we investigate the tech helping driverless cars communicate on the roads. We'll learn some useful new words and phrases, and as usual you'll find all the vocabulary on our website, bbclearningenglish.com.

**Phil**: But first, I have a question for you, Pippa. As you mentioned, we don't have self-driving cars here in London yet. So, which was the first city to have fully driverless robotaxis on the streets? Was it:
a) Philadelphia,
b) Phoenix, or
c) San Francisco?

**Pippa**: I'm going to say b) Phoenix because I think it's quite a car-friendly city.

**Phil**: OK. Well, we will find out the answer, but at the end of the programme. Because accidents are often caused by not seeing the oncoming danger, Professor Brewster wants to make driverless cars more visible. He discussed his work with BBC Tech Life's presenter Chris Vallance.

**Chris Vallance**: What kind of signals worked in terms of **alerting** pedestrians and runners and cyclists to the intentions of the vehicle?

**Stephen Brewster**: We tested two things. We tested **animations** and fixed, coloured light rings. The **animations** – they work well for pedestrians, but when it came to runners and cyclists, they have much less time to make their decisions, so if you do a quick **glance** at a vehicle, you don't really see the animation.

**Pippa**: It's important to **alert** people to driverless cars. To **alert** somebody means to warn them about a potential danger. Some cars do this with **animations** – displays of moving images. For example, a green man walking to signal it's safe to cross.

**Phil**: Animations alert pedestrians but are not suitable for runners and cyclists, who are travelling too fast to react. They only have time for a **glance**. A **glance** at something means a quick look. So, instead Stephen's team used coloured light rings, similar to traffic lights, to warn cyclists and runners.

**Pippa**: Culture is another problem. The meaning of body language can change. For example, hand waving can mean 'please proceed' in one culture, but 'thank you' in another.

**Phil**: Ammar Al-Taie is a researcher at the Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in South Korea. Here, Ammar tells BBC World Service's Tech Life why Korean culture is especially suited to self-driving cars.

**Ammar Al-Taie**: In South Korea, for example, **jaywalking** is illegal, whereas in the UK, you know, we tend to **jaywalk** a lot.

**Pippa**: **Jaywalking** is an American English word. It means crossing the road at a place where crossing is not allowed or without taking care. Unlike Britain, jaywalking is rare in South Korea, making the country safer for autonomous cars.

**Phil**: We've heard a lot about making driverless cars safer, and with plans to introduce them to London later this year, we'll soon see how safe they really are.

**Pippa**: Now, isn't it time to reveal the answer to your question, Phil?

**Phil**: I asked you which city was the first to have self-driving cars on the road, and you said it was Phoenix... and that is the correct answer. **Phoenix, Arizona**, was the first city in the world to have self-driving cars operating on public roads without a human safety driver, and that was in 2017. Let's recap the vocabulary we've learned, starting with **eye contact** – looking directly into someone's eyes.

**Pippa**: To **wave** at someone is a way of saying hi, by raising your hand and waving it from side to side.

**Phil**: If you **alert** someone, you warn them of potential danger.

**Pippa**: An **animation** is a display made up of moving images.

**Phil**: A **glance** is a quick, brief look at something.

**Pippa**: And finally, the American English term **jaywalking** means crossing the road where you're not supposed to. Once again, our six minutes are up. If you'd like to hear more programmes about technology, you'll find plenty on our tech topic page, all on our website. And there you'll also find a quiz and worksheet for this episode. Visit bbclearningenglish.com. We'll see you again soon, but for now it's goodbye.

**Phil**: Goodbye!
